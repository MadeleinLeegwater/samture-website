Who this is for

You do not have to be an engineer. You do have to want to build.

The craft is called knowledge crafting, and it is not the property of one profession. The people who turn out to be good at it come from marketing, policy, operations, finance, research and engineering. What they have in common is not a background but a daily practice: they work with information where it matters whether it is right, and they want to put something up themselves that works.

The standard measures what you have built, not where you came from. That does not make it gentler but stricter: a background you have, a working foundation you have to show.

Part one asks for no programming experience. You set up your own working environment, step by step, with guidance alongside. Part two goes deeper and becomes more technical — that is where you learn to model and to query. Anyone who finishes part one knows for themselves whether they want to take that step.

What you actually do

Eight themes, and what each one leaves you with.

Each theme is four lessons: one that sets the question of the week, two that carry the material, and one worked session where we build it together. Around forty minutes of video, plus your own building — reckon on roughly four hours a week, most of it on your own system.

You build from the first week, and you begin at the bottom: identity and permissions first, then connections, then behaviour. What you put up in that first week is not a demonstration but the bottom layer.

Setting it up properly

The working environment itself, in the order a foundation is laid: identity and permissions first, convenience second.

You end with a running environment, the same shape for everyone.

What does it need to know?

The questions your system has to be able to answer, collected from the people who do the work rather than invented at your desk.

You end with fifteen to twenty-five questions that become your measuring stick.

What may go where

Data landscape, classification, residency, landing zones. The theme nobody else teaches, and the one your security officer cares about.

You end with a data plan you can hand to your own CISO.

What your work is made of

Things and relationships. What is an object and what is merely a property of one, and why “relates to” is never good enough.

You end with an entity and relationship map of your own domain.

It learns who you are

Context, conventions and memory that survive between sessions, so you stop re-explaining your own organisation every morning.

You end with a system that knows how your work is put together.

Connecting to your real tools

Your mail, your files, your systems — with least privilege from the first connection, and anything that sends, deletes or pays asking first.

You end with a connected environment with rights you can defend.

Making it do the work

The first real task that leaves your hands. Not all of it: the part the machine does well, with you where the judgement sits.

You end with one working automation on your own process.

What may it decide, and can you prove it?

Provenance and auditability. Where it came from, when, on whose authority — and where you draw the line on what an agent may decide.

You end with an auditable system, and the wall where part two begins.

What “meaning” actually means

Knowledge does not begin with data. It begins with identity.

The moment you can say that two mentions are the same thing, you stop having text about things and start having things you can ask about. One ordinary sentence, climbing that ladder:

As prose

“The services agreement with our Jebel Ali logistics supplier was signed on 7 July and runs for two years.”

A person can read it. A machine can find the characters.

As a value

2026-07-07

True, and useless. Nothing owns it.

As information

signedOn: 2026-07-07

Now it means something — inside one document, and nowhere else.

As knowledge

Agreement → Organisation → Person

The date belongs to an agreement, which belongs to a supplier, which has an owner. Nobody wrote that down. It follows.

The bar

A certificate of competence, not of completion.

Most certificates in this field are issued for finishing the material: watch enough of it and one arrives. That measures consumption and prints competence.

The Samture competence standard is the same standard every builder working on a Samture foundation is measured against. There is one, and it holds for both routes.

  1. It is awarded on work, not attendance: a finished system you built yourself, assessed against a published standard by someone who reads it.
  2. It expires. A mark of competence that cannot lapse says nothing three years later.
  3. It can be withdrawn. At the level of practice, that is precisely what makes it worth holding.

You are not learning from one person.

Madelein leads the programme and is there every week. On the parts where it becomes genuinely technical — modelling, the semantic layer, connecting real source systems — specialists join her: people who do that work daily and bring in what a course otherwise misses. What it looks like when the data does not agree with itself, when the permissions turn out to be wrong, when the client changes their mind halfway through.

The cohort itself is the third source. Everyone builds in the same shape, so you can read each other’s work and say something useful about it. That is why the shape is the same for everyone, and why the group stays small.
